The Learning Assistants were holding study sessions outside on benches and in overcrowded dining halls. The location changed week to week, which meant students who wanted to come sometimes couldn't find them. The program was growing fast and it had nowhere to go.

That conversation with CEILS staff started the STEM Collaborative Learning Center.

UCLA's Center for Education Innovation and Learning in the Sciences (CEILS) ran a Learning Assistant (LA) program pairing trained undergraduate LAs with large introductory science, engineering, and math courses. Pilot studies had shown that attendance at LA-supported sessions correlated with significantly higher grades. The program was expanding and it needed more space. When I heard about this challenge, I saw the opportunity: the Science and Engineering Library's Research Commons was heavily used in some hours and underutilized in others. 

Working with fellow science librarians, Access Services staff, and our campus partners, we put together a proposal that was straightforward in principle but initially harder to move forward in practice. Some library stakeholders initially read the proposal as "giving away space" where the proposal actually said "maximizing it." The early conversations involved a lot of words like "ceding" and "losing." Getting past that required reframing the question from "whose space is this?" to "what is the purpose and mission of this space?" Co-articulating the mission of the space around "supporting student success" fundamentally shifted the conversation.

The project team agreed to repurpose an existing space with minimal budget impact, using furniture already on hand. The total materials cost was $785.76 (four stanchions, two mobile whiteboards, and two poster sign holders to define the space and support the new configuration). Research on active learning environments supported the low-tech approach: studies in the field had shown that flexible seating and writable surfaces mattered more to student success than expensive technology. We planned the space accordingly, using usage headcount data to determine how much of the room could be dedicated to LA sessions while still protecting open study space for students who needed it.

We opened the STEM CLC in September 2017.

Within the first year, the results exceeded all expectations. The number of Learning Assistants increased from 90 per quarter to 238; instructor participation more than doubled, from 7–10 to 20–25 courses per quarter. More than 20% of all students served through the LA program attended sessions in the STEM CLC. In Fall 2017 alone, more than 5,000 students had access to collaborative office hours outside of class time. The Science and Engineering Library's overall space usage increased 17%, from 68,199 visits in 2016–17 to 79,524 in 2017–18. Over the project's duration, students logged 19,510+ hours of peer-to-peer collaborative learning in the space. Eighty-six percent of students who attended workshops and collaborative office hours agreed or strongly agreed that those interactions helped them learn the material.

The STEM CLC also prompted change beyond the space itself. The Sciences Libraries developed a new Learning Spaces Policy that allowed sharing our learning spaces with campus groups focused on scholarship and research, with special consideration for learning communities that lacked official space support. The CLC's success attracted donor interest in improving spaces within the Science and Engineering Library. It also modeled a service approach that spread: inspired by the CLC, other campus departments began offering collaborative office hours in their own spaces, and the LA program more than doubled the total students it reached — from 10,818 to 24,874 — as that expansion took hold. Campus-wide exposure to collaborative learning increased by 30–50%.

The space kept growing. In Winter 2019, the UCLA Undergraduate Writing Center added a dedicated service point in the STEM CLC — the first on the south side of campus near the STEM departments — with a 92% appointment fill rate in its first two quarters. In Winter 2020, the Library added an embedded research assistance service point staffed by graduate students from the Information Studies program.

The project gave me a close look at what it takes to help drive campus-wide change from inside a library. The internal stakeholder work — rewriting policy, reframing purpose, building the case incrementally — made the space design itself possible. And partnering with CEILS and PEERS meant co-designing around programs that were already working and whose data on equity and student achievement made the library's value concrete in a way that traditional library metrics often struggle to do. This was a case where focusing on a shared mission turned out to be the most impactful thing we could do together as campus partners.
